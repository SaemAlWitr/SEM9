### Government vs. Governance
-   Governance emerges when regulation requires expert knowledge
    -   Experts define the conditions under which something can be done
    -   This shifts authority from elected government to technocratic bodies
-   Key claim: governance structures rest on a foundation of legitimacy, not just legality
### Expertise and Regulation: The Milk Example
-   Soviet milk production used to illustrate how governance requires expert standards
    -   Quality of milk cannot be assessed by observation alone; requires technical criteria
    -   Standards set limits on inputs (e.g., hormones, painkillers like diclofenac)
-   Diclofenac case: painkiller given to cattle to increase milk yield

### Legality vs. Legitimacy
-   Legal: permitted by law or government order
-   Legitimate: accepted as morally valid by society
-   Examples used:
    -   Delhi police pellet use on students: legal (standing order existed), but widely seen as illegitimate
    -   Nazi Germany: killings carried out through legal mechanisms, but clearly illegitimate
-   Relevance: governance claims legitimacy through expertise, but the two can diverge sharply

-   Traditional model: command-and-control (state as sole regulator)
-   Shift: regulation now happens through multiple actors, not just government
    -   Supermarkets regulate by placement, selection, and organic-only stocking
    -   Other countries banning Indian mangoes due to high pesticide levels: private and state actors both regulating
-   This shift theorized by Julia Black (2002) as “polycentric regulation”
    -   Same regulatory practices, but new framework for understanding who regulates

### What Is Technology? Revisiting the Definition
-   Three categories of technology definition covered in last class:
    -   Technology as hardware
    -   Technology as rules (algorithms, software, behavioral systems)
    -   Behavioral technology: no material form, yet shapes human action
-   Key debate: is technology necessarily “applied science”?
    -   Common first response: yes, technology = application of science
    -   Problem: leads to conceptual and historical contradictions
### Historical and Conceptual Problems with “Technology = Applied Science”
-   Historical problem: significant technology predates modern science
    -   Modern science (control experiment + mathematical formalization) is at most ~400-500 years old
    -   Vast technological history exists before it emerged
-   Conceptual problem: assumes science is already settled and stable
    -   Implies a firm, consensus-based body of theory simply waiting to be “applied”
    -   Implies all problems are solvable by asking scientists
### Science as Fragile, Not Settled
-   Science is not a fixed body of truth; it is fragile and contested
    -   Causality claims (“X causes Y”) are theoretical, not absolute
    -   Data does not speak for itself: it is collected, organized, and interpreted
-   History of the last 40-75 years shows repeated failures of the “science is in control” assumption
    -   Unintended consequences are endemic to modern technological deployment
    -   Regulatory language of safety (e.g. “0.001% risk”) masks underlying uncertainty
### Why This Matters for Governance and Regulation
-   If technology = applied science, then safety regulation defaults entirely to scientists
    -   Regulators ask scientists; scientists claim control; disasters still happen
    -   Example: pesticide regulation, genetic modification safety assessments
-   “Unintended consequences” as a concept signals the breakdown of this model
    -   Systems claim control, then reframe failures as unintended rather than foreseeable
-   Polycentric governance (“poly-sense”) vs. command-and-control model
    -   Contemporary governance involves multiple centers of regulatory norms and actions
    -   This complexity is obscured when technology is reduced to applied science
### Technology Leading Science: Reversing the Arrow
-   Sometimes newer technology generates new scientific understanding, not the other way around
    -   Nanotech helping understand nanoscale phenomena
    -   Human genome mapping: the mapping project itself drove understanding of genetics
-   Key takeaway: the relationship between technology and science is bidirectional
    -   Assuming technology merely applies pre-existing science is both historically and conceptually inadequate

### SCOT Framework: Social Construction of Technology
-   Core claim: to understand technology, look beyond design to the social relationships that make it possible
-   Technical change is not an inexorable march toward efficiency; efficiency is an attribution, not an inherent property
-   Biker/Bijker’s bicycle example:
    -   Multiple viable designs coexisted, preferred by different social groups
    -   ~80-90 years of push and pull between groups before convergence
    -   The “winning” design is retrospectively read as the most efficient, but it wasn’t objectively so
-   Bulb example sharpens the point: power, not efficiency, drove the outcome
-   David Noble’s corollary: many factory design changes were about owner control over labor, not efficiency gains
-   Attribution runs across domains: need vs. want, scientific taxonomy (Pluto), causality (Latour’s pharaoh/E. coli example)
### Artifacts Have Politics (Winner)
-   Next article to read: “Do Artifacts Have Politics?” by Langdon Winner
-   Bridges from SCOT’s claim (design reflects social relationships) to a stronger one: artifacts can embed politics independently
-   Two ends of the debate:
    -   SCOT extreme: change the power relationship, the technology must change
    -   Opposite extreme: technology is fixed; everything else must change around it
-   Winner’s argument sits between these poles, demonstrating two ways artifacts carry politics:
    1.  Design can impose order on a public issue (e.g., Moses’s Long Island parkway bridges: low clearance bars buses, restricting beach access to car owners, effectively by race and class)
    2.  (Third point to be covered next class)
-   Public issues are normally resolved through law and deliberation; Winner’s point is that design can pre-empt or override law
### Examples of Power Structuring Design
-   Urban space: Haussmann’s narrow Parisian streets made barricades harder; churches designed to inspire awe
-   Anti-homeless design: subway benches and spaces engineered to prevent sleeping
-   Building accessibility: ramps absent for decades; disability groups campaigned for years before law changed (India: first law ~1996 after 1994 Panchgani conference)
-   Delhi roads: 20%+ of land given to roads, structured around car owners; bus lanes introduced for Commonwealth Games 2010, then dismantled within 18 months under pressure
-   Delhi Metro: subsidized fares opened city-center public space (Lodhi Garden) to people who previously had no access to leisure or open space
-   Bus vs. metro entry design: buses allow entry without a ticket by design; metros are architecturally closed, enforcing payment structurally rather than legally

  - “Efficiency” is itself a product of social agreement, not an objective fact

  - Multiple actors (GE, antitrust division, utility companies) shaped the final design
  - Tesla vs. Edison framed as different engineering traditions, not just personalities
- SCOT school (Social Construction of Technology)
  - Network of relationships makes any design possible in a particular way
  - Technological closure is engineered through consent, not pure efficiency
- Efficiency as agreement, not truth
  - The question is not “is it efficient?” but “how did we come to agree it is efficient?”
  - Parallels drawn to science: science as agreement about truth, not truth itself
  - Examples: cochlear implants, genetic theory, double helix (Watson and Crick’s use of Rosalind Franklin’s work)
  - Mendel’s work ignored for 40 years; gene imagery is a theoretical construct, not reality
### Shoshana Zuboff: Surveillance Capitalism (Video)
- Zuboff framed as a leading philosopher of the 21st century; coined “attention economy” / “surveillance capitalism”
  - Work sits at the intersection of psychology, capitalism, and technology
  - Tied to Winner’s argument: technology is not neutral; it embeds political and economic values
- Behavioral surplus: the core mechanism
  - Data collected exceeds what is needed to improve services
  - Residual “data exhaust” (search errors, click patterns, typing speed) used to build predictive behavioral models
  - Predictions sold to business customers, not users; users are the raw material, not the customer
- Surveillance extends beyond online advertising
  - Facebook contagion experiments: subliminal cues altered real-world emotions and behavior without user awareness
  - Pokemon Go revealed as a surveillance capitalism product incubated inside Google
    - Businesses paid for “lure modules” to drive real-world footfall (Starbucks, McDonald’s)
    - Players had no idea they were being herded to paying establishments
  - Facial recognition models trained on Facebook photos sold to military operations, including those used to track Uyghurs in China
- Key misconceptions Zuboff challenges
  - “I have nothing to hide” misunderstands what data is actually collected
  - Disclosed personal data is the least important part; residual behavioral data is far more revealing
  - Supermarket case: algorithms detected pregnancy before the girl’s own father knew, via shampoo purchase patterns

# Two Cores

**Time limit per test:** 2 seconds  
**Memory limit per test:** 256 megabytes

Two processor cores, numbered $1$ and $2$, must divide $n$ tasks between them. Task $i$ has a processing time of $a_i$.

The cores assign the tasks in order, from task $1$ to task $n$. Core $1$ makes the first decision.

For each task, the core making the decision must choose one of two actions:

- **Take the task:** assign it to itself. The other core makes the decision for the next task.
- **Give the task:** assign it to the other core. The same core makes the decision for the next task.

Each core wants to maximize the sum of the processing times of the tasks assigned to it. Both cores know the entire array and play optimally.

Determine the final total processing time assigned to each core.

## Input

The first line contains a single integer $n$ ($1 \le n \le 10^5$) — the number of tasks.

The second line contains $n$ integers $a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n$ ($0 \le a_i \le 10^9$) — the processing times of the tasks.

## Output

Print two integers: the total processing time assigned to core $1$ and the total processing time assigned to core $2$, assuming optimal play.

## Example

**Input**

```text
3
4 9 2
```

**Output**

```text
9 6
```

## Note

Core $1$ gives the first task, with processing time $4$, to core $2$. Core $1$ keeps the next decision.

Core $1$ takes the second task, with processing time $9$. Core $2$ then makes the next decision and takes the last task, with processing time $2$.

The final totals are $9$ for core $1$ and $4+2=6$ for core $2$.
